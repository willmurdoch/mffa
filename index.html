Our story

A few laps around the park
became a movement.

2020

A diagnosis at 22

Jack, a 22-year-old from Downingtown, PA, is diagnosed with spinocerebellar ataxia — a rare, progressive neurological disease that attacks coordination and balance. Within a year, his symptoms progress so quickly that he is using a wheelchair full-time.

2021

Three wheels, one spark

A neighbor living with the same rare condition introduces Jack to the recumbent trike. That spring, Jack manages a few careful laps around the local park — and feels something he hadn't felt since his diagnosis: mobility on his own terms.

2023

From laps to century rides

The laps become miles. The miles become long-haul rides. Jack builds up to riding as far as 100 miles at a time — proof that with the right machine, ataxia doesn't get the last word on movement.

2024

Moving Forward for Ataxia is born

Jack and his mom Monica turn their rides into a mission: every mile ridden raises money to buy trikes for other people with ataxia. Their most ambitious campaign takes them 1,500 miles from Maine to Ohio — 41 days in the saddle, over 55,000 feet of climbing.

Today

17 trikes and counting

In under two years, this tiny grassroots charity has raised more than $55,000 and delivered 17 trikes to riders across 13 states — and as far away as Cyprus. The mission keeps moving forward. The next trike could be yours to give.

Understanding the disease

What is ataxia?

Ataxia means a loss of coordination. In degenerative ataxias, the cerebellum — the brain's movement control center — slowly atrophies. Walking, balance, speech, swallowing, even eye movement become harder and harder. It affects roughly 150,000 people in the United States, and there is no cure.

⚖️

Balance & gait

Standing, walking, and cycling on two wheels become unsafe. Falls are constant. Many people move to wheelchairs within a few years.

💬

Speech & swallowing

The same loss of muscle coordination affects speaking clearly and swallowing safely — isolating people from everyday life.

📉

Progressive, incurable

Most hereditary ataxias steadily worsen over time. There is currently no cure and very little that medicine can offer.

🚴

Movement is the medicine

The one thing shown to help: staying active. Exercise is the primary treatment goal — it helps preserve function, strength, and quality of life for as long as possible.

Why recumbent trikes

Three wheels
change everything.

You can't fall off a machine that can't tip over. A recumbent trike replaces the balance ataxia has taken with engineering — and gives back real, independent, heart-pounding exercise.

🛡️

Stability by design

Three wheels and a low center of gravity mean no balancing required — the machine holds you. Falling simply isn't part of the equation.

🪑

A seat that supports

The reclined seat cradles the whole body, and feet secure to the pedals — so riders with limited trunk control can push hard and ride far in comfort.

❤️

The best available therapy

Serious cardio, leg strength, and coordination work — the exact exercise neurologists ask ataxia patients to get, in a form they can actually do.

🕊️

Freedom, returned

For someone who has given up their car keys and their two-wheeler, a trike is independence: open trails, wind, distance — joy on their own terms.

The catch: a fitted adaptive trike costs $3,000–$6,000+

Insurance almost never covers it. For someone on a fixed disability income, that price tag means "never." That's exactly the gap this charity exists to close.

How it works

From your donation
to the open road.

  1. 1

    You give

    Every dollar you give goes straight into the trike fund — no middleman, no overhead skimmed off the top. Give in seconds with a card or a PayPal account.

  2. 2

    We find a rider

    We connect with people living with ataxia across the country (and beyond), and work with them to choose and fit the right adaptive trike for their body and their goals.

  3. 3

    A life starts moving

    The trike arrives free of charge, and someone who thought their riding days were over is back outside — exercising, exploring, and moving forward.
